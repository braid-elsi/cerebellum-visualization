## 1. Background "Tonic" Firing (Baseline Activity):
Purkinje cells are always active at rest — even when you’re not moving! They naturally fire at a steady rate of about 30–100 spikes per second. This constant activity provides a baseline level of inhibition to the deep cerebellar nuclei.

This tonic firing sets the stage for fine-tuning movements.
Changes in the firing rate (increasing or decreasing) are what actually encode movement corrections.

## 2. Contextual Firing (Driven by Parallel Fibers):
Parallel fibers — the long axons of granule cells — carry sensory and motor information from mossy fibers. Each Purkinje cell gets input from hundreds of thousands of parallel fibers.

When a specific pattern of parallel fibers fires, it creates a simple spike in the Purkinje cell.
Simple spikes occur at high frequencies **(50–100 Hz)** and are thought to encode ongoing movement, sensory context, or motor intentions.
Example: As you reach for a cup, parallel fibers signal the position of your arm, the visual location of the cup, and the motor commands being sent to your muscles. Purkinje cells modulate their firing rate in response to these signals.
In this context, Purkinje cells are adjusting inhibition to the deep nuclei, helping refine movement.

## Spencer's Notes
In general, expectations are learned through experience and often exist within the synapses between neurons. Expectations can occur on different timescales and evidence suggests that different synaptic and circuit-level specializations are used for creating short-term (hundreds of milliseconds) and long-term expectations (days to years). For short-term expectations, our current hypothesis is that parallel fiber synaptic facilitation fulfills this role because it results in a rapid adaptation of Purkinje cells to sensory input that is repeated or sustained.

For long-term expectation, the "expectation memory" is also likely held at parallel fiber synapses but in a more persistent manner. Interestingly, the effect of LTD at parallel fiber synapses -- namely the **increased propensity for Purkinje cells to pause their firing** -- affects the activity of climbing fibers through an important trisynaptic loop: 
1. Purkinje cells **inhibit** nucleo-olivary neurons
2. Nucleo-olivary neurons **inhibit** the inferior olive (the neurons that give rise to climbing fibers)
3. Climbing fibers **excite** the Purkinje cells

In short, what happens is that with LTD at the parallel fiber synapse, Purkinje cells:
1. Learn to pause their firing under specific contexts in an adaptive manner (i.e. to promote a corrective output from the cerebellar nuclei), 
2. The pause in firing disinhibits nucleo-olivary neurons so that they fire more
3. The nucleo-olivary neurons inhibit the inferior olive such that the climbing fibers are less likely to fire during the context in which the motor error has been corrected. 

I like to think of the increased inhibition to the inferior olive as the expectation. 

It's also important to mention that even prior to learning, the neurons of the inferior olive already have some idea about what is expected and what is not. Climbing fibers fire primarily in response to strong sensory inputs. From the perspective of motor learning this makes sense because sensory input that is associated with a motor error can be quite strong (remember my example of tripping and falling). And when sensory inputs are strong, the cerebellum notices and starts making adjustments to how actions are performed in order to reduce them. As it learns, it suppresses the activity of neurons of the inferior olive, making climbing fiber firing less likely.
